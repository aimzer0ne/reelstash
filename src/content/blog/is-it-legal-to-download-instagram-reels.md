Downloading a public Instagram Reel to watch offline is a personal use that rarely causes problems. What gets people into trouble is what they do with the file afterwards: reposting it, editing it into their own videos, or using it to sell something without the creator's permission. The creator owns the copyright in their Reel, and posting it publicly does not change that.

*This guide is general information, not legal advice. Copyright law differs from country to country. If you plan to use someone else's video commercially, ask a lawyer where you live.*

## Who owns an Instagram Reel?

The person who made it. In most countries, copyright exists automatically the moment a video is created; nothing has to be registered. When creators post on Instagram, they keep ownership and give Instagram a license to host, show, and distribute the video on its apps. That license is for Instagram, not for other users.

Music is a separate layer. Songs in Reels are licensed to Instagram for use inside the app. That license does not travel with a copy you download, which is one reason why reposting a Reel with music on another platform can get it muted or taken down.

## Watching offline vs. reusing: where the line is

| What you do | How risky it is | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Save a public Reel to watch later | Personal use; very low risk in practice | Keep the file private |
| Save your own Reels as a backup | You own them | Go ahead |
| Send the file to a friend in a private chat | Gray area | Send the Instagram link instead |
| Repost it on Instagram, TikTok, or YouTube | Needs permission; can lead to takedowns and strikes | Ask the creator first and tag them |
| Use it in an ad, product, or monetized video | Needs a license, often for the music too | Get written permission |
| Use a short clip in a review or commentary | May qualify as fair use or fair dealing | Use only what you need and add your own commentary |

Saving is not the problem; distribution is. Copyright protects a creator's right to decide where their work appears and who profits from it.

## Does giving credit make reposting legal?

No. Tagging the creator, writing "all rights to the owner," or adding "no copyright intended" is polite, but none of it grants you any rights. Copyright is about permission, not attribution. Many creators are happy to say yes if you ask, and a repost they agreed to is better for both of you.

## What Instagram's rules say

Instagram's Terms of Use say you must not post content that violates someone else's intellectual property rights. Rights holders can report a repost, Instagram removes infringing content, and accounts with repeated violations can be disabled.

Instagram itself treats saving public Reels as normal. Its own **Download** option in the share menu lets anyone save a public Reel unless the creator turns it off. The saved copy carries a watermark with the creator's username, which shows where the line sits: keeping a copy is fine, passing it off as yours is not.

For reuse inside Instagram, the platform gives you tools that keep the creator credited automatically: **Remix**, sharing the Reel to your Story, or sending it in a DM. These are always safer than downloading and re-uploading.

## What about fair use?

In the United States, fair use allows limited use of copyrighted work without permission. Courts weigh four factors: why you use it (commentary, criticism, teaching, or parody count in your favor), what kind of work it is, how much of it you use, and whether your use replaces the original in the market. The UK, Canada, India, Australia, and many other countries have narrower *fair dealing* rules for purposes such as criticism, review, and news reporting.

A short clip with your own commentary on top is far more likely to qualify than reposting a whole Reel as it is, which almost never does. Fair use is decided case by case, so treat it as a defense, not a plan.

## How to ask a creator for permission

A short, specific DM works best:

> Hi! I loved your Reel about [topic]. Could I share it on my account @yourname? I'll tag you in the caption and won't edit it or use it in ads.

Screenshot the reply in case you need it later. For commercial use, such as an ad or a client project, get permission in writing with the terms spelled out, and check who owns the music.
